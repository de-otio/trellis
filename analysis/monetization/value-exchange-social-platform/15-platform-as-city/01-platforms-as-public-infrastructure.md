# Lineage 1 — Platforms as Public Infrastructure

The political-theory and legal-scholarship lineage that argues large platforms have *already* become public infrastructure and should be governed accordingly. This is where the "platform is the city" framing lives as a normative claim.

---

## Core Works

| Author / Work | Year | Contribution |
|---|---|---|
| **Benjamin Bratton, *The Stack: On Software and Sovereignty*** (MIT Press) | 2015 | Platforms as a new geopolitical layer with sovereign-like functions. Originates "platform polity" framing. |
| **K. Sabeel Rahman, "The New Utilities: Private Power, Social Infrastructure, and the Revival of the Public Utility Concept"** (Cardozo Law Review) | 2018 | Legal argument that platforms have become essential infrastructure and should be regulated as public utilities. The closest existing argument for tax-funded common services. |
| **Julie Cohen, *Between Truth and Power: The Legal Constructions of Informational Capitalism*** (OUP) | 2019 | Platforms as quasi-sovereign actors operating in a legal regime they partly write themselves. |
| **Mike Masnick, "Protocols, Not Platforms"** (Knight First Amendment Institute) | 2019 | Federation/open-protocol as governance model for digital public space. The technical-architecture sibling of the public-infrastructure argument. |
| **Eli Pariser & Talia Stroud, "Civic Signals" / New_Public** | 2020– | What healthy digital public space would look like, in deliberately civic-design terms. |
| **Ethan Zuckerman, Institute for Digital Public Infrastructure (UMass Amherst)** | 2022– | The most active US programme treating platforms as public infrastructure rather than private goods. Coins "digital public infrastructure" (DPI) as a category. |
| **Hertie School, Centre for Digital Governance (Berlin)** | ongoing | Active EU-side research on platform governance. Relevant for DACH positioning. |

---

## What This Lineage Gives the Hypothesis

Strong support for **claim 1**: yes, platforms exhibit public-infrastructure characteristics, supply common services with non-rival / hard-to-exclude properties, and are reasonable to treat as a kind of public space.

Some support for **claim 3**: Rahman in particular argues the utility-regulation precedent applies, which historically has involved cross-subsidised, levy-funded service obligations.

---

## What This Lineage Does Not Give

A self-funded operational model. The political-theory side argues the analogy holds and then prescribes regulation **by the actual state** — DSA, antitrust, common-carrier rules — not self-funded common services *inside* the platform.

It also says almost nothing about **claim 2** (B2B/B2C co-citizenship). Where B2B participants appear, they tend to appear as the regulated party (advertisers, sellers), not as fellow citizens of the same polity.
