# Lineage 3 — Platform Cooperativism

The governance-side lineage that redistributes platform surplus via co-ownership rather than via levy. Adjacent to the hypothesis but not strictly tax-shaped: cooperativism uses *ownership* as the redistributive mechanism, where the hypothesis uses *taxation*. Worth surveying because the underlying intent — surplus stays with the participants, not with absentee capital — is the same.

## What This Lineage Gives the Hypothesis

Practical experience with **non-VC, non-ad-extractive** platform models. Platform co-ops have operated in e-commerce, cleaning services, culture work, finance, software, and transportation. They establish that the participants-fund-the-platform model is viable for some workloads.

It also names the failure modes Scholz himself identifies: **competition, financing, regulation, education, member involvement, and identity**. Several of these (financing in particular) are directly relevant to whether the platform-as-city model can survive against ad-funded incumbents.

---

## What This Lineage Does Not Give

A **tax model**. Cooperativism is an *ownership* model. Surplus is redistributed by virtue of co-ownership — dividends, lower fees, governance rights — not by a levy on activity. The two are not interchangeable: a tax can fund services for non-owners; ownership cannot.

It also tends to be **single-class**: worker-owned, or user-owned, or producer-owned. The hypothesis's mixed B2B/B2C co-citizenship is *closer* to a multi-stakeholder co-op (the Mondragon model, broadly) than to most platform co-ops in the literature, but the multi-stakeholder pattern is acknowledged as harder, not easier, by Scholz himself.
