# 01 · The argument

The external position, source-faithful. Trellis mapping is in

## Why age limits are a "dead end"

Age limits target the wrong layer. The real harm is **algorithmic control of
attention**, not the mere presence of minors on a platform. Watzl argues the
age-limit debate is *"precisely the kind of distraction the companies are hoping
for"* — it imposes almost no constraint on the business model while looking like
decisive action.

Three reasons age limits fail:

1. **Rights cost** — pushing youth off platforms without safe alternatives cuts
   them out of public/democratic discourse.
2. **Privacy cost** — age verification creates a *new* harm (Australia's regime
   pushes toward facial scanning to access Facebook). The brief cites EFF on the
   surveillance cost of age verification.
3. **Ineffectiveness** — minors migrate to whatever isn't formally labelled
   "social media"; companies spin up services that dodge the category. The
   mental-health evidence for bans is "scant" — recent meta-analyses find no
   clear causal link between social-media use as such and mental-health decline.

Intellectual lineage: Herbert Simon's 1970s "scarcity of attention" and James
Williams (ex-Google strategist) on the "liberation of human attention" as a
moral struggle.

## "Social media" is the wrong category (brief §4.1)

There is no coherent "social media" category to regulate — the same overwhelming
attentional power lives in search (Google), messaging (WhatsApp), shopping
(Amazon), and chatbots (ChatGPT). The harm is the *mechanism* — personalised
engagement-ranking, micro-targeted / real-time-bidding advertising, and
manipulative design ("dark patterns") — not the platform type. Regulation should
target these mechanisms wherever they occur.

## The harm is broader than mental health (brief §3, §4.3)

The mental-health framing is both empirically shaky and strategically wrong.
Concentrated attentional power threatens:

- **autonomy** (self-determined life),
- **epistemic justice** (what one can know),
- the **ability to find meaning** in life,
- **social recognition**,
- **meaningful democratic participation**, and
- **digital sovereignty**.

Attention is "a core component of personhood and agency." The remedy is therefore
**empowerment, not restriction** — dispersing and regulating attentional power
and enhancing user agency ("cognitive liberty").

## The five proposed alternatives (brief §5)

1. **Regulate the attention mechanisms** — personalised ranking/recommendation,
   micro-targeting / real-time-bidding ads, dark patterns — irrespective of
   platform category.
2. **Mandate transparency and accountability** — disclose how recommenders work,
   what they optimise for (watch time, CTR); auditable access for researchers.
3. **Enforce existing regulation** — competition law, EU DSA/DMA, UK OSA against
   monopoly, self-preferencing, lock-in, and data aggregation that enables
   micro-targeting.
4. **Promote interoperability and user mobility** — portability, and switching
   or leaving platforms *without losing social networks*.
5. **Invest in public-interest digital infrastructure** — public/non-profit,
   ad-free, open and accountable recommenders, and platforms specifically
   designed for children or teenagers.
